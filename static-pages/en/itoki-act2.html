01 — Prologue

The chair that answers a very modern question

As hybrid and remote work became the norm, the seat we spend the most hours in — the one at our own desk — quietly became the most important chair in our lives. The Itoki Act2 was designed for exactly that gap between the boardroom chair and the kitchen-table chair.

The Act2 doesn't sell itself with a celebrity designer's name or loud marketing. Instead, it speaks through the accumulated perspective of Itoki — a Japanese company that has done nothing but build working environments for more than 130 years — and through an in-house development team that spent years obsessing over a single act: sitting. That is also why we feature it. We look for chairs with a reason to exist, not just a price tag.

03 — The brand

A story that began in 1890

Itoki's history starts in December 1890 in Osaka, with a small shop called Itoki Shoten. Its founder was Itō Kijūrō; the name "ITOKI" comes directly from his surname (Itō) and given name (Ki). A person's name became a brand.

Itoki was never just a furniture seller. In 1903 it imported and popularized the stapler in Japan; in 1908 it set up its own manufacturing arm; in 1913 it developed Japan's first cash register. It split off its manufacturing in 1950 and reunified manufacturing and sales in 2005 as today's ITOKI Corporation. Across more than a century, one thread runs through everything Itoki has made — tools that help work flow better in an office. To understand the Act2, it helps to remember that Itoki is less a "furniture company" than a company that has been designing how people work.

04 — Philosophy

"Omotenashi" — anticipating the need before it's spoken

Itoki's long-held stance is clear: furniture is not "something that fills a space" but "a device that changes behavior." A single desk or chair shapes the focus and the fatigue of the person in front of it.

Underneath that stance are two distinctly Japanese ideas. One is omotenashi (おもてなし) — the spirit of wholehearted hospitality that anticipates a guest's needs before they are voiced and attends even to what will never be seen. The other is monozukuri (ものづくり) — the craft ethic of "making things properly," finishing even an unseen component with an artisan's care. This is why the best Japanese furniture can feel commanding without ever showing off.

Read through that lens, every choice in the Act2 reads differently. Rather than adding more levers, the seat simply follows your movement as you lean — an act of omotenashi that spares the user from ever thinking about "settings." Filling the hidden interior of the seat with a urethane-free, breathing material engineered to resist sagging through 150,000 compressions — that is the quiet obstinacy of monozukuri, caring for the user years down the line. The Act2 proves its standing not through a flashy exterior, but through the care poured into the places you cannot see. That is its idea of Japanese premium.

True hospitality never advertises what it has done for you. The sitter simply has nothing to think about — and that invisible ease is where omotenashi is complete. — on the spirit running through the Act2

So Itoki's development always starts from human movement. People never hold one posture — they lean, twist, cross their legs, pull themselves forward. A good chair, in this view, doesn't fight those movements; it follows them. That principle runs through every feature of the Act2 below.

05 — Lineage

From Vertebra to Act

Itoki's chair lineage includes a landmark name. In the early 1980s Itoki brought the ergonomic chair "Vertebra," designed by Emilio Ambasz and Giancarlo Piretti, to the Japanese market. The idea that "the backrest should move with the spine as you sit" was radical at the time, and the chair went on to be regarded as a milestone in design history. In 2019 Itoki revived the concept as "vertebra03" with designer Fumie Shibata.

Vertebra matters not because it is an old classic, but because the way of thinking it embodied — treating a chair as "a structure that responds to movement" rather than a fixed support — became the starting point of Itoki's chair design. That question echoes through the Good Design Gold-winning "Spina" (2007), the iF- and Red Dot-recognized "FLIP FLAP" (2017), and the 2018 "Act." The Act2 is the most recent answer in this long line — and it is notable for shifting the center of gravity of that answer from "mechanism" to "the material of the seat."

06 — The makers

A chair by people who had never designed a chair

The most telling detail in the Act2 story is that the people who made it are not star designers. Yoshihiro Teramoto, who led the original Act (2018), had never designed a chair before. He says it was precisely because he wasn't a chair expert that he could ask the user's questions again — "Why is a chair this complicated to adjust? Why does it feel stuffy after sitting a while?"

Development of the Act2 passed to Kensuke Takahashi of the product-development division. Rather than outsourcing the design, they worked with Itoki's internal research and CMF (color-material-finish) teams to, over several years, re-design the act of sitting itself. The Act2 feels uncluttered because it was designed to solve a user's problem, not to make a splash.

"A chair is not the protagonist — it should be the supporting actor that holds up the person doing the work." — on the philosophy behind Itoki's Act
07 — Concept

The question the Act2 sets out to answer

The original Act (2018) was built on the idea of "Adjust & Active" — adjust freely, and work in motion — an answer for a moment when people were starting to work without fixed desks.

Seven years on, the Act2 goes a step deeper. In an age where everyone's sitting environment is different — hybrid, remote, solo offices — the Act2 aims at "a new comfort built around each individual's way of sitting." It rests on four themes: always comfortable, stress-free to operate, kind to the environment, and pleasant to look at. From here we look at how that promise is actually built into the chair's structure.

08 — Mechanism ①

A chair that pivots from your ankles

The Act2's recline works on a different principle from most chairs. A typical chair pivots the backrest alone around a point behind the seat, so leaning back lifts your gaze and your hips shift. The Act2 is designed to tilt seat and back together, pivoting near the natural axis of a seated body — around the ankles.

As it reclines, the seat and back move together in roughly a 1:3 ratio — as your back goes back, the front of the seat supports you so your body doesn't slide. Maximum recline is about 20°, lockable in five steps, with the spring tension set by a lever. It lets you move between "lean back and breathe" and "pull in and focus" in a single motion.

09 — Mechanism ②

It supports the pelvis first, not just the lower back

The reason a long sit hurts is usually that the pelvis collapses backward and the lower back rounds. The Act2 targets exactly this, splitting back support into two points: the pelvis (sacrum) and the lumbar. It braces the pelvis first, then supports the lumbar above it, with adjustable height — a step up from the original Act's single lumbar support.

To be candid, the Act2's lumbar is more "a flexible support that follows you" than "a firm pad that pushes." If you prefer a strong, assertive lumbar push, this is a point of personal preference — something we address openly in sections 14–15.

10 — Mechanism ③

A seat that breathes: "Respitec"

The seat is where Itoki invested the most, and it is what sets the Act2 apart. Most office-chair seats use urethane foam — soft, but stuffy, prone to sagging over time, and hard to recycle.

The Act2 uses a urethane-free seat material called "Respitec," built in two layers: an upper fiber cushion ("Fibre Cushion VL" by Teijin Frontier) and a lower three-dimensional spring mesh ("Breath Air®" by Toyobo). Breath Air is a structure of fine resin fibers entangled in mid-air — rather than pressing down like foam, the 3D springs push back as they support you, with air moving freely through the whole thing. Hence a seat that, quite literally, breathes.

The durability is striking: by Itoki's measure, the seat shows 0% sag after 150,000 compression cycles, against roughly 20% for typical urethane under the same test. It's a structural answer to the common complaint that "a seat sags after a few years."

11 — Mechanism ④

Adjustments that fall to hand

A good feature you can't reach easily is a feature you stop using. The Act2 places its key adjustments where your hands naturally rest.

4D link arm

The armrests move four ways — forward/back, in/out, height, and angle. In particular, the arms pull in close to the body, so you can keep your arms near you while typing and ease the load on your shoulders. At this price, a 4D arm is a real strength.

Seat-depth adjustment

The seat slides to set depth across roughly 37.0–44.5 cm, so you can match it to your leg length and avoid pressure behind the knees — a small thing that matters a lot over a long sit.

Head support (optional configuration)

The head-support model adjusts about 60 mm in height and tilts ~30°, cradling the neck and head — noticeable when you lean back to rest.

12 — Sustainability

Designed-in, through material choice

The Act2's environmental story comes from material design, not slogans. Both the surface and cushion layers of the seat are thermoplastic resins, achieving the material-level recyclability that urethane could not. "Last a long time (0% sag), and be recyclable at the end" — durability and recyclability aimed at together.

In fairness: what Itoki officially states for the Act2 is "urethane-free, recyclable material design" and high durability. We did not find separate third-party eco-certification marks (GREENGUARD, ISO, etc.) in public material. If certification matters to you, please ask before buying.

14 — For whom

Who it suits

The Act2 is for people who change posture often rather than sit frozen in one position. It particularly suits those who lean and pull in repeatedly, those bothered by seat heat and sag over long hours, and keyboard-heavy users who want to fine-tune arm position. With a seat that drops to 45 cm, it also fits relatively smaller builds with feet flat on the floor.

Great for

  • Remote / hybrid workers sitting in one chair all day
  • Anyone sensitive to seat heat and sagging — Respitec's strength
  • Those who need a 4D arm to ease arm/shoulder load
  • Smaller statures needing a low seat height

Worth knowing first

  • Lumbar is gentle/flexible — may feel light if you want a strong push
  • Some users note the hips slide slightly when reclining deeply
  • 5-step lock isn't infinitely fine
  • Footprint is on the larger side — check tight spaces / doorways
  • Seat depth isn't generous for sitting cross-legged
15 — Comparison

Where it sits

The Act2's Japan list price (from ¥132,240) places it in the upper-mid tier. Its closest peer in the Japanese market is the Okamura Sylphy, at a near-identical price. Globally, it sits just above the Herman Miller Sayl and at roughly half the price of a loaded Aeron.

16 — Verdict & care

Our take, and the warranty

What we liked most about the Act2 is the seat. Spend long enough around chairs and you learn that what a user feels every day isn't a grand mechanism — it's "a seat that stays cool and doesn't sag after years." The Act2 takes that ordinary-but-hard problem head-on, through material. That it's the work of in-house engineers focused on the user's problem, rather than a celebrity designer, is part of why we trust it.

Warranty · care

  • Warranty · The Itoki Act series uses a tiered warranty (appearance / moving parts / structural). The Act2's exact term and scope are confirmed at the point of authorized sale — please ask before purchase.
  • Care · Wipe the seat mesh with a dry or lightly damp cloth. Avoid prolonged direct sunlight.
  • Configuration · Back type (elastomer/mesh), head support, and color change the spec and price.

FAQ

Q. How is it different from the original "Act"?
A. The Act2 is the 2025 successor to the 2018 Act; the key upgrades are the urethane-free "Respitec" seat and two-point pelvis+lumbar support. Itoki notes some new parts are compatible with the original Act.

Q. Does it auto-adjust to my weight?
A. No. The Act2's adjustments (recline tension, seat depth, arms) are all manual. There is no "sit down and it sets itself" feature.

Q. I'm shorter — will it fit?
A. The seat drops to about 45 cm, so smaller builds can sit with feet flat. The head support may sit a little high for very short users.
