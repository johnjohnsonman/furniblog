01 — Prologue

What if there's no single "right" way to sit?

Most ergonomic chairs start from a premise: there is one correct posture, and the chair's job is to hold you in it. Generation by Knoll starts by rejecting that premise entirely. People lean, twist, perch, sit sideways, fold a leg up, swing around to talk to a colleague — and a chair, this one argues, should simply move with all of it.

Launched in 2009 and designed by the New Zealand studio Formway, Generation became one of the most awarded task chairs of its era precisely because it didn't try to discipline the sitter. It's a chair from Knoll — the house that brought European modernism to America — that treats freedom of movement as the ergonomic feature. This is our full, honest read on what it does, how it works, and where it does and doesn't fit.

03 — The maker

Knoll — modernism, since 1938

Knoll was founded in New York in 1938 by Hans Knoll, with a mission to bring Bauhaus and European modernist ideals to American interiors. When Hans married Florence Schust in 1946, the firm became Knoll Associates; Florence Knoll went on to direct the legendary Knoll Planning Unit and effectively invent the modern corporate interior.

Knoll's catalog reads like a design-history syllabus: it produces Mies van der Rohe's Barcelona chair, Eero Saarinen's Womb and Tulip chairs, and Harry Bertoia's Diamond chair. That pedigree is the backdrop to Generation — a working chair from a company that has always treated furniture as serious design. In 2021 Herman Miller acquired Knoll, and the two now operate as MillerKnoll — which is why a Generation and an Aeron now share a single catalog: two very different answers to the same question of how to sit well.

04 — The designers

Formway — the studio from Wellington

Generation was designed by Formway Design, a New Zealand studio founded in 1956 that began collaborating with Knoll in 2002. Formway is known for a research-led approach — studying how people genuinely behave before drawing a single line.

That method produced Generation's founding observation, stated plainly by Formway: the basic assumption of office seating — that there is an optimal way to sit — is "fundamentally flawed." Workers, they noted, spend their day "shifting, stretching, rotating, leaning, and reclining." Rather than fight that reality, Formway set out to design a chair in which, as Knoll put it, "every imaginable posture could be deemed ergonomically correct."

Formway describes the result as "the world's first multi-mode, multi-posture task chair" — the product of years spent studying not just anthropometric averages but behavior: how real people fidget, swivel, perch, and quietly break the rules of "proper" sitting. That behavioral focus is what separates Generation from chairs that simply pile on adjustment range; it set out to remove the need to adjust at all.

05 — The premise

A chair designed around movement, not a single posture

This is the heart of Generation. Knoll explicitly names the postures it set out to support — leaning in, reclining, swiveling, "perched," and "side saddle" (sitting sideways with a leg draped over an arm). Where a conventional chair would resist these as "wrong," Generation is engineered to accommodate them as normal.

The consequence is a chair with deliberately few controls. Instead of a wall of levers asking you to dial in the "correct" setup, Generation uses materials and geometry that respond to your body automatically. As Knoll frames it, the goal is "intuitive movement that takes the work out of sitting." Whether that minimalism is liberating or limiting depends on the sitter — a tension we'll address honestly later.

"The basic assumption about office seating — that there is an optimal way to sit — is fundamentally flawed." — Formway Design, on the thinking behind Generation
06 — Technology ①

The Flex Back Net

Generation's back is not woven mesh and not foam-and-fabric. It's a single molded high-performance elastomer — the "Flex Back Net" — shaped in a distinctive "figure-8" geometry. Crucially, its flex is material- and geometry-driven, not mechanical: there are no springs or pivots in the back. The elastomer simply bends, then springs back, as you move.

Because the back can flex side to side as well as front to back, it follows you when you twist or lean to one side, and four independent "flexors" act like suspension across multiple directions of movement. One independent reviewer described the material as bending "back upon itself when pressure is applied" — it gives a lean-back stretch that few task chairs can match.

The absence of a mechanical back carries a quiet advantage: there are no springs, hinges, or linkages in the backrest to loosen, squeak, or wear out — the support is the material. The trade-off, which we'll return to, is that its firmness is set by the elastomer's engineering rather than by a dial; you can't soften or stiffen the back to taste.

07 — Technology ②

Dynamic Suspension — recline with no tension knob

Here's where Generation diverges most from a typical premium chair. Its recline — Knoll's "Dynamic Suspension" — is automatic and weight-compensated, built from the same elastomer family "without the complexity of metal links or springs." The chair reads your weight and balances the recline for you.

What that means in practice: there is no traditional infinite tilt-tension dial. You don't crank a knob to match your body weight — the chair does it. The only recline controls are a stepped tilt-preference selector (a few ranges) and a tilt lock. For someone who hates fiddling with settings, this is the point; for someone who loves precise manual tension, it's a real philosophical difference.

Note for accuracy: some retailer listings mention a "tension knob" for Generation. That appears to be copied from the lower-cost sibling "ReGeneration by Knoll," which does use one. Generation's own recline is the knob-free, weight-compensated system described here.

08 — Technology ③

How it lets you sit sideways

The party trick of Generation — genuinely sitting sideways or perched — is enabled by the back, not the arms. Three features work together:

  • Flex Top — the top edge of the backrest folds back up to ~90°, becoming a comfortable armrest (or headrest) when you sit sideways or informally.
  • Flex Seat — the seat edge flexes through about 270°, so you can sit forward, perched, or off to one side without a hard front lip digging in.
  • Continuous Lumbar — support carries around the chair so your back is still supported even when you're turned sideways.

An independent reviewer described sitting fully sideways with legs over an armrest, using the backrest itself as a side rest — exactly the use case Generation was built for.

09 — Technology ④

Arms & adjustments — minimal by design

The arm supports are cantilevered (not a "flex" mechanism). On armed models the basic arm adjusts in height only; the upgraded High Performance arm adds width (~2"), depth (~1.5"), and pivot (~45°). Here's the honest adjustment map:

So Generation deliberately offers fewer granular adjustments than, say, a Steelcase Leap. That's the design thesis — let materials adapt instead of asking you to. Just know it going in.

10 — Sustainability

Materials & certifications

Generation's frame uses burnished cast aluminum and glass-reinforced nylon supports, with the elastomer Flex Back; the base is aluminum or reinforced nylon. It's made in the USA at a LEED Gold–certified Knoll facility and carries a 12-year warranty.

On sustainability, the numbers are worth dating precisely, because they changed over time:

  • Recycled content · ~20% today (7% pre- + 13% post-consumer), per the 2025 NSF EPD. (Older 2009–2011 marketing cited 38–44%; the current authoritative figure is 20%.)
  • Recyclability · up to 88% at end of life (2025 EPD)
  • Standards · meets ANSI/BIFMA X5.1 (rated for a 10-year lifetime); has a published NSF Type III EPD
  • GREENGUARD · the current Knoll page states "Greenguard-certified" (no tier named)

In fairness: retailers also claim "GREENGUARD Gold" and "BIFMA LEVEL 3," but we could not confirm those tiers on the certifiers' own databases — so we don't state them as fact. We also found no Cradle to Cradle or SCS Indoor Advantage certification for this chair.

12 — For whom

Who it's for

Generation is for "movers" — people in connected, collaborative offices who switch tasks and devices, swivel to talk, perch to read, and recline to think. If you rarely hold one posture, Generation's whole design works in your favor. If, on the other hand, you sit dead-still facing forward for eight hours and want to micro-tune every parameter, an independent reviewer's advice applies: test-sit it first, because its strengths may not be your needs.

13 — Comparison

Where it sits

At roughly $1,630–$1,705 new, Generation sits just below a loaded Herman Miller Aeron, overlaps the Steelcase Gesture, and prices above a base Steelcase Leap or Herman Miller Sayl. Bought refurbished (~$300–$550), it becomes one of the more affordable routes into a genuinely high-end chair.

14 — Honest take

Strengths & trade-offs

Strengths

  • The flexing elastomer back is the standout — superb for lean-back stretching and moving
  • Genuinely supports side-sitting, perching, swiveling — built for active offices
  • Premium, solid build ("feels expensive")
  • No-fuss recline — nothing to dial in

Trade-offs (from independent reviews)

  • Seat foam can feel firm / develop "dead spots" on long, static sits
  • The back material can grab or pull clothing at the lower back
  • Lumbar is minimal/continuous — less targeted than rivals
  • Fewer granular adjustments than a Steelcase Leap
  • Premium price (though refurbished units soften this a lot)
15 — Recognition

Awards & standing

Generation arrived to strong recognition in 2009. It won a GOOD DESIGN Award (The Chicago Athenaeum, 2009) — confirmed on the awarding body's own winners list, crediting Formway and Knoll. It is also widely reported to have taken Best of NeoCon Gold for task seating (2009) and an Interior Design "Best of Year" (2009).

For accuracy, we don't claim awards we couldn't verify: there's no record tying Generation to the IDEA, Red Dot, or iF awards, so we don't list them. (Watch for name collisions with the unrelated "Walter Knoll" company and Knoll's separate "Spark" chair.)

16 — Verdict & FAQ

Our take

Generation by Knoll is one of the most genuinely original task chairs of the last two decades. Where most ergonomic chairs add controls, it removes them and lets a clever elastomer back do the adapting — and for people who move, fidget, perch and swivel, that feels like freedom. It is not the chair for someone who wants a plush seat, a strong targeted lumbar, or a knob for everything; in those cases a Steelcase Leap or a padded chair will suit better. But as a premium chair for an active, modern way of working — and especially as a refurbished value buy — it remains a standout.

Good to know

FAQ

Q. Do I set the recline tension to my weight?
A. No. Generation's recline is automatic and weight-compensated — there's no tension knob. You only set a stepped tilt preference and a lock.

Q. Can I really sit sideways in it?
A. Yes — that's a core design goal. The Flex Top folds to ~90° as a side armrest, the seat edge flexes ~270°, and lumbar support continues around the chair.

Q. How is it different from "ReGeneration by Knoll"?
A. ReGeneration is a separate, lower-cost chair in the same family (and it does use a tension knob). This page is about the higher-end Generation.

Q. Is it adjustable like a Steelcase Leap?
A. No — Generation is minimal by design, relying on its flexing materials instead of many manual controls. If you want maximum granular adjustment, the Leap offers more.
