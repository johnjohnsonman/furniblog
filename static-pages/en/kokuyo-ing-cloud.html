01 — Prologue

What if a chair moved with you, not against you?

Sitting still is the problem. Hold one posture for hours and the body stiffens, circulation slows, focus drifts. Kokuyo's "ing" series was built on the opposite idea — a seat that keeps gliding gently in every direction so your body never fully stops moving. The ingCloud is the third and most advanced chapter of that idea, and Kokuyo frames it with an unusual phrase: a chair that feels "closer to wearing than to sitting."

Kokuyo even put numbers to it: in its own testing, four hours of gentle swaying on an ing chair burns about as many calories as a 1.5 km walk. Launched in December 2025, the ingCloud is a Japanese flagship that took years to develop, and it arrived to one of the design world's top honors. This is our full read on what it is, how its gliding mechanism works, the evidence behind the wellness claims, and who it's really for.

03 — The maker

Kokuyo — Osaka, since 1905

Kokuyo was founded in 1905 in Osaka by Zentarō Kuroda, beginning with covers for Japanese-style account books. The "KOKUYO" brand name was adopted in 1917. Today it's one of Japan's largest makers in two worlds at once: stationery — its Campus notebooks are a national staple — and office furniture and workspace design, which it entered in 1960.

That dual heritage matters. Kokuyo doesn't approach a chair as a standalone object so much as part of "how a workplace works," and it brings a stationery-maker's obsession with everyday, in-the-hand detail to its furniture. Now a publicly listed company marking its 120th anniversary, Kokuyo has the scale and the research depth to spend years on a single chair mechanism — which is exactly what the ing series represents.

04 — The "ing" idea

The chair that refuses to hold you still

To understand the ingCloud, you have to understand the original "ing" (2017). Its breakthrough was a seat that glides — tilting forward, back, sideways and diagonally through a full 360° — riding on a two-layer mechanism that uses gravity, not springs, to both let you sway and gently return you to center. When you stand, a stopper locks it.

The point is "active sitting": the chair invites constant, tiny, unconscious movements so the body keeps working even while seated. The original ing went so far as to drop a conventional fixed lumbar support entirely, on the logic that the gliding motion itself helps the spine find its natural curve. It's an idea closer to a balance ball than to a traditional task chair — and it earned Kokuyo a Red Dot "Best of the Best" in 2018 and a German Design Award in 2019.

A note on a common claim: the ing is often called "the world's first 360° gliding chair." Kokuyo's own wording is more careful ("an industry-first way to shape how we sit"), and we couldn't independently verify a literal "world's first," so we don't state it as fact.

05 — Three generations

From ing to ingCloud

The ing idea evolved across three chairs. The original ing (2017) proved the gliding seat. ingLIFE (2022) brought it to a wider, lighter audience. The ingCloud (2025) is the flagship — the most ambitious expression of the concept yet, developed over roughly eight years with digital workers (engineers, programmers) interviewed as the target users.

Where the original ing asked the sitter to actively drive the motion, the ingCloud aims to disappear — to follow your unconscious micro-movements and continuously redistribute pressure so the support feels almost weightless. Kokuyo's framing is deliberately provocative for an office chair, which we look at next.

06 — Concept

"Closer to wrapping yourself in than to sitting"

Kokuyo describes the ingCloud not as something you sit on but something you are wrapped in — まとう (matou), "to wear." The ambition is a sensation close to weightlessness: a chair that envelops the body and moves with it rather than presenting a fixed surface to sit against.

It's a strong claim, and like all such claims it's best tested in person. But it isn't empty marketing — it maps directly onto two concrete engineering changes over the original ing: gliding extended to three points of the chair, and a frameless mesh back. Those are the substance behind the poetry, and we take them in turn.

07 — Technology ①

Triple Gliding

The original ing glided mainly at the seat. The ingCloud extends gliding to three points — the seat, the backrest, and the armrests — so the whole chair moves as a coordinated system when you shift. Kokuyo calls the combined system the "3D Ultra Auto Fit Mechanism."

As with the original, the motion is driven by gravity rather than mechanical springs: the chair stores and returns energy through its geometry and your own weight, swaying with you and easing you back toward center. Because the back and arms now move too, leaning or turning doesn't break your contact with the chair — support follows you instead of staying put. It's the difference between a chair that tolerates movement and one engineered around it.

08 — Technology ②

The 3D Hammock Mesh back

The ingCloud's back is a frameless "3D Hammock Mesh." Conventional mesh chairs stretch the mesh across a rigid side frame; removing that frame lets the back cradle the body three-dimensionally and flex with the gliding motion rather than fighting it. The result is the "hammock-like," enveloping feel the chair is named for.

Kokuyo backs that up with its own body-pressure mapping for the ingCloud: it reports a peak seat pressure of about 61, against roughly 174 on a conventional chair — the load spread broadly across the body instead of concentrating under the sit bones.

Note that, in keeping with the ing philosophy, this is not a chair built around a strong, adjustable, push-into-your-lumbar pad. Support comes from the way the mesh wraps and the way the whole chair glides with you — a fundamentally different approach to back comfort than, say, an Okamura Sylphy with its adjustable back curve. Whether that suits you depends on what your back wants (more on this in the comparison).

09 — Technology ③

How it all fits together

Triple Gliding and the 3D Hammock Mesh are bundled into Kokuyo's "3D Ultra Auto Fit Mechanism" — the name for the chair adapting to you continuously and automatically, without you setting much of anything. That's the throughline of the whole ing series: rather than asking you to dial in the "correct" posture, the chair keeps quietly adjusting to the posture you're actually in.

The trade-off, common to this design philosophy, is fewer manual controls than a heavily-adjustable Western task chair. The ingCloud's published adjustments are modest — seat-height adjustment and an optional headrest — with the rest handled by the mechanism. If you love fine-tuning every parameter by hand, that's worth knowing upfront.

10 — The evidence

The case for moving while you work

The ing series rests on "active sitting" — the idea that the human body, with its 360-plus joints and roughly 700 muscles, is built to move, and that small, continuous motion while seated beats rigid stillness. What sets ing apart from chairs that merely allow movement is that Kokuyo went and measured the effect. Its testing on the ing chair produced the figures it now builds the whole series around:

  • It turns sitting into gentle exercise. Kokuyo found that four hours of swaying desk work on ing is equivalent to walking about 1.5 km — roughly 85 kcal, or about 840 kcal over a working week (which Kokuyo cheerfully likens to six mugs of beer).
  • It keeps your muscles switched on. In EMG testing with Keio University's Sports Medicine Research Center, simply sitting on ing engaged the shoulder muscles in 80% and the lower-back muscles in 50% of participants, versus a conventional chair.
  • Relaxed, yet focused. In EEG testing, 60 minutes of gentle swaying raised concentration-linked beta waves in 60% of people and relaxation-linked alpha waves in 70% — the "calm but alert" state everyone wants at a desk.

These are Kokuyo's own studies — small panels, conducted with academic input from researchers at Keio and Waseda — rather than independent peer-reviewed trials. But they are real measurements behind a real mechanism, and they're a large part of why the ing concept earned its following. The ingCloud inherits that same active-sitting DNA, refined here for all-day, near-weightless comfort.

12 — For whom

Who it's for

Kokuyo developed the ingCloud explicitly for digital workers — engineers, programmers, anyone who sits at a screen for very long stretches and benefits from staying subtly in motion. If you're a fidgeter, a leaner, a poster-shifter, the gliding system works with your instincts instead of against them.

Great for

  • Long-session desk workers who stiffen up sitting still
  • People who like to move, sway, and shift while they work
  • Those who want a chair that adapts automatically, not via many levers
  • Buyers who value a distinctive, award-recognized Japanese design

Worth knowing first

  • Flagship pricing — well above mainstream premium task chairs
  • Support comes from glide + mesh, not a strong adjustable lumbar pad
  • Few manual adjustments by design — not for tinkerers
  • Weight capacity and several spec figures aren't published yet
  • So new that long-term, independent reviews are still scarce
13 — Comparison

Where it sits

At roughly ¥238,000–258,000 (excl. tax), the ingCloud is a true flagship — about 1.7–2× the price of mainstream Japanese premium chairs. The original ing (around ¥130,000–144,000) actually overlaps those rivals; the ingCloud sits a clear tier above them.

The honest framing: the ingCloud isn't really a head-to-head rival to a Sylphy or an Act2 on price — it's a tier above, and it asks you to value a specific thing they don't fully offer: a chair built around continuous, multi-point motion rather than a fixed, well-supported posture. If you want a strong adjustable lumbar and forward tilt for focused desk work, the Sylphy or Act2 are more conventional (and far less expensive) answers. If the idea of a seat that quietly moves with you all day is what draws you, the ingCloud is the most fully realized version of it.

According to Kokuyo's announcement, the ingCloud won a Red Dot Design Award 2026 "Best of the Best" — the top tier — in the Innovative Design category, with a reported score of 96/100. Kokuyo notes it's the second "Best of the Best" for the ing series, after the original ing's 2018 honor.

16 — Verdict & FAQ

Our take

The ingCloud is one of the most genuinely interesting office chairs of its generation — a flagship that doubles down on a real, distinctive idea (keep the body moving) rather than chasing more adjustment knobs. The Triple Gliding system and frameless hammock mesh are substantive, not just slogans, and the "wearing, not sitting" feel is a real sensation worth trying. The honest caveats are its flagship price, its deliberately minimal adjustability, support that comes from motion rather than a firm lumbar, and the simple fact that it's brand-new — so sit in one before committing, and treat any precise wellness statistics with skepticism. For the right person — a long-hours digital worker who loves to move — there's nothing quite like it.

FAQ

Q. How is the ingCloud different from the original ing?
A. It's the third-generation flagship. It extends gliding from the seat to three points (seat, back, arms), adds a frameless 3D hammock mesh back, and aims for a near-weightless "wrapping" feel rather than the original's more active, user-driven motion.

Q. Does it have a strong adjustable lumbar?
A. No. The ing philosophy relies on the gliding motion and the wrapping mesh for back support rather than a strong, separately adjustable lumbar pad. If you need firm targeted lumbar, consider an Okamura Sylphy or Itoki Act2.

Q. What's the weight capacity?
A. Kokuyo has not published the ingCloud's weight capacity. (For reference, the original ing was rated to 110 kg — but we won't assume the ingCloud matches it.)

Q. Is it available outside Japan?
A. It launched in Japan in December 2025; international availability may be limited and is best confirmed before purchase.
